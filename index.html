    Start · First principles

    Five ideas that explain most of biomed

    Learn these first. Almost every pathway in this atlas is a variation on one of them, which is why understanding beats memorising here.

    1. Negative feedback keeps values in range

    A sensor detects a change, a control centre responds, and an effector pushes the value back. Low blood calcium is sensed by the parathyroid glands, PTH is released, and calcium rises until PTH switches off. Disease often means a broken loop: a parathyroid tumour keeps making PTH even when calcium is high.

    2. Every builder has a breaker

    Osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Clotting and fibrinolysis. Pro-inflammatory Th1 and Th17 against regulatory T cells. Health is the balance; disease is one side winning. Many drugs work by tipping the balance: bisphosphonates slow the breakers, anticoagulants slow the builders.

    3. Signal → receptor → second messenger → response

    Hormones, neurotransmitters and cytokines all work this way. Know the four receptor families (ion channel, G protein-coupled, enzyme-linked, intracellular) and you can predict how fast any signal acts and where a drug could block it.

    4. Calcium is the thread through both blocks

    Calcium triggers neurotransmitter release at the NMJ, unlocks actin for contraction, is clotting factor IV, and is what bone stores. Low calcium therefore causes tetany, arrhythmia and, in theory, poor clotting; the body defends it tightly (2.2–2.6 mmol/L).

    5. Structure decides function and failure

    Collagen type I resists tension, so bone and tendon use it; cartilage type II resists compression. Gram-negative bacteria have an outer membrane, so some antibiotics cannot reach their target. When you meet a new structure, ask what it is built to resist.

    Think of it as

    A thermostat. Biomed questions are usually "what does the thermostat sense, what does it switch on, and what happens if the switch sticks?" Hypercalcaemia, anaphylaxis and haemophilia are all stuck switches.

    Collagen types

    • Type I: bone, tendon, ligament, skin, mature scar. Resists tension. Faulty in osteogenesis imperfecta.
    • Type II: hyaline and elastic cartilage. Resists compression.
    • Type III: reticular fibres, blood vessels, early granulation tissue (replaced by type I as a scar matures).
    • Type IV: basement membranes.

    Mnemonic: "Be So Totally Cool, Read Books": Bone (I), Cartilage (II), Reticulin (III), Basement membrane (IV). Vitamin C is needed to make collagen, which is why scurvy causes bleeding gums and poor wound healing.

    Fluid compartments and osmolarity

    • Osmolarity is the number of solute particles per litre; mostly made by electrolytes.
    • Na⁺ is the main extracellular cation; K⁺ the main intracellular one.
    • Plasma has more protein (albumin) than interstitial fluid, so it has a slightly higher oncotic pressure. This pulls fluid back into capillaries (see Starling forces in the lymphatic section).
    • Tonicity decides which way water moves: in a hypotonic solution red cells swell and burst (haemolysis).

    Ways cells talk

    • Direct (juxtacrine): through gap junctions or surface contact.
    • Paracrine: to neighbouring cells (e.g. histamine from mast cells).
    • Autocrine: a cell signals itself (e.g. IL-2 driving T cell proliferation).
    • Endocrine: hormone through the blood (e.g. PTH).
    • Synaptic: neurotransmitter across a synapse (e.g. ACh at the NMJ).

    Signals must switch off: phosphodiesterase breaks cAMP down to AMP; acetylcholinesterase breaks down ACh.

    Enzymes and how drugs inhibit them

    • Competitive: competes for the active site; overcome by more substrate.
    • Non-competitive: binds an allosteric site whether or not substrate is bound; lowers Vmax.
    • Uncompetitive: binds only the enzyme–substrate complex.
    • Irreversible: forms a covalent bond. Aspirin acetylates COX; organophosphates phosphorylate acetylcholinesterase.
    • Feedback inhibition: the end product inhibits the first enzyme of its own pathway at an allosteric site.
    Chapter 2 · Musculoskeletal biomed

    Bone, calcium and muscle

    Bone is living tissue that is constantly broken down and rebuilt. Calcium hormones decide the balance. Muscle turns a nerve signal into movement through a calcium-triggered chain of events. Learn the pathways and the diseases explain themselves.

    MSK · Bone tissue

    What bone is made of

    Bone is a composite, like reinforced concrete: collagen fibres are the steel rods that resist bending and tension, and calcium hydroxyapatite crystals are the concrete that resists compression.

    Matrix

    • Inorganic (about two-thirds by weight): calcium hydroxyapatite crystals. Hardness and resistance to compression; the body's calcium and phosphate store.
    • Organic (about one-third): osteoid, mostly type I collagen with proteoglycans. Flexibility and resistance to tension.
    • Take away the mineral (osteomalacia, rickets) and bone bends; take away good collagen (osteogenesis imperfecta) and it snaps.

    Compact vs spongy bone

    • Compact (cortical): dense osteons (Haversian systems) of concentric lamellae around a central canal with vessels, joined by Volkmann's canals. Forms the shaft and outer shell; about 80% of skeletal mass.
    • Spongy (trabecular, cancellous): a lattice of trabeculae lined up along lines of stress, with red or yellow marrow between them. Fills epiphyses and the inside of short, flat and irregular bones. Higher turnover, so it is lost first in osteoporosis (vertebrae, wrist, hip).

    Bone shapes

    • Long (femur, metacarpals): levers.
    • Short (carpals, tarsals): stability and small movements.
    • Flat (sternum, skull vault, scapula): protection and broad muscle attachment.

    • Red marrow: haematopoietic tissue. In adults: axial skeleton, girdles, proximal ends of femur and humerus.
    • Yellow marrow: mostly fat; fills long-bone shafts in adults. Can convert back to red after severe blood loss.

    Link to the immune block: every blood and immune cell begins in red marrow.

    MSK · Remodelling and repair

    Remodelling and fracture healing

    About a tenth of the adult skeleton is replaced each year. Remodelling repairs microdamage, reshapes bone to load and releases calcium on demand. Fracture healing is the same machinery switched to emergency mode.

    Fracture healing

    Primary vs secondary healing

    • Secondary (indirect) healing happens when there is a little movement at the fracture (casts, splints, intramedullary nails). It goes through the callus stages and is the usual route.
    • Primary (direct) healing needs anatomical reduction and absolute stability (compression plates and screws). Osteons simply grow across the gap ("cutting cones"); no callus forms.

    What slows healing

    Poor blood supply (scaphoid, femoral head, talus), movement at the fracture, infection, smoking, diabetes, malnutrition, long-term steroids, older age. Complications: delayed union, non-union, malunion, avascular necrosis, infection, compartment syndrome, fat embolism, VTE.

    Describing a fracture

    Describe a fracture in this order: open or closed; bone and side; location (diaphysis, metaphysis, intra-articular); pattern; displacement, angulation, rotation and shortening. Causes: traumatic (normal bone, big force), stress (normal bone, repeated small forces), pathological (weak bone, small force: osteoporosis, metastases, myeloma).

    Calcium and phosphate homeostasis

    Why PTH dumps phosphate

    Calcium and phosphate precipitate together as calcium phosphate. If PTH released calcium from bone without getting rid of the phosphate that comes with it, the two would simply bind again. Flushing phosphate into the urine leaves more calcium free in the blood.

    Measured calcium

    About 45% of plasma calcium is free (ionised, the active form), about 45% is bound to albumin and about 10% is complexed with anions. Labs report adjusted (corrected) calcium for the albumin level. Alkalosis increases binding to albumin and lowers ionised calcium: that is why hyperventilation causes tingling and carpopedal spasm.

    Hypocalcaemia (<2.2 mmol/L): "CATs go numb"

    • Convulsions, Arrhythmias (long QT), Tetany, numbness and paraesthesia around the mouth and fingers.
    • Signs: Chvostek's (tapping the facial nerve twitches the face) and Trousseau's (a BP cuff causes carpal spasm).
    • Why: low calcium makes nerve membranes more excitable, so they fire spontaneously.
    • Causes: vitamin D deficiency, chronic kidney disease, hypoparathyroidism (often after thyroid or parathyroid surgery), low magnesium, hyperventilation (ionised calcium only).

    Hypercalcaemia (>2.6 mmol/L): "bones, stones, groans, moans, thrones"

    • Bones (pain), stones (kidney stones), groans (abdominal pain, nausea, constipation), moans (low mood, confusion, fatigue), thrones (polyuria and thirst).
    • Why: high calcium stabilises membranes, so nerves and gut muscle become sluggish; the kidney cannot concentrate urine.
    • Causes: primary hyperparathyroidism and malignancy (bone metastases, multiple myeloma, PTH-related peptide) account for about 90%; also excess vitamin D, thiazides, sarcoidosis.

    MSK · Pathology

    Bone disease

    Most bone diseases are either too little bone, poorly mineralised bone, bone made badly, or bone infected or invaded.

    Osteoporosis: too little bone

    • What: low bone mass and broken-down microarchitecture (thin, disconnected trabeculae), so bones break with minimal trauma.
    • Why: resorption outpaces formation. Oestrogen normally restrains osteoclasts, so the menopause accelerates loss.
    • Risk factors: female, age, early menopause, low BMI, smoking, alcohol, long-term glucocorticoids, family history of hip fracture, rheumatoid arthritis, immobility.
    • Fractures: vertebral compression (height loss, kyphosis), distal radius (Colles'), hip. Often silent until the first fracture.
    • Diagnosis: DEXA scan T-score ≤ −2.5 at the hip or spine. Fracture risk estimated with FRAX or QFracture.
    • Treatment: a bisphosphonate (oral alendronic acid first line), with calcium and vitamin D if intake is low; denosumab or others if bisphosphonates are unsuitable; falls prevention and weight-bearing exercise.

    Osteomalacia and rickets: soft bone

    • What: osteoid is made but not mineralised, usually because of vitamin D deficiency. Called rickets in children (before growth plates fuse) and osteomalacia in adults.
    • Causes: little sunlight, darker skin at UK latitudes, covering clothing, poor diet, malabsorption (coeliac disease), liver or kidney disease, some anti-epileptic drugs.
    • Rickets: bowed legs, wide wrists, "rickety rosary" (swollen costochondral junctions), delayed walking.
    • Osteomalacia: bone pain, proximal muscle weakness (waddling gait), Looser's zones (pseudofractures) on X-ray.
    • Treatment: vitamin D (colecalciferol) and calcium.

    Paget's disease: bone made chaotically

    • Overactive, giant osteoclasts followed by frantic, disorganised osteoblast repair: bone is large, thick but weak. Usually over 50; skull, pelvis, spine, femur, tibia.
    • Bone pain, bowing of the tibia, enlarging skull (hats no longer fit), deafness (nerve compression), pathological fracture, rarely osteosarcoma.
    • Bloods: very high ALP, normal calcium and phosphate. Treated with bisphosphonates.

    Osteogenesis imperfecta: bad collagen

    • Inherited defect in type I collagen (usually autosomal dominant COL1A1/COL1A2): "brittle bone disease".
    • Many fractures from minor trauma, blue sclerae, hearing loss, dental problems, short stature. Severity ranges from mild to lethal before birth.
    • Important differential when a child has multiple fractures and abuse is being considered.

    Bone infection

    • Osteomyelitis: infection of bone, most often Staphylococcus aureus. Spreads through the blood in children (metaphysis of long bones) and from wounds, fractures or diabetic foot ulcers in adults.
    • Septic arthritis: a hot, swollen, very painful joint with fever. An emergency: aspirate before antibiotics, because bacterial enzymes destroy cartilage within days.

    Bone invaded by cancer

    • Metastases are far commoner than primary bone tumours. Five cancers that spread to bone: breast, prostate, lung, kidney, thyroid.
    • Multiple myeloma: a plasma cell cancer that makes osteoclast-activating factors: lytic "punched-out" lesions, hypercalcaemia, anaemia, kidney damage ("CRAB": Calcium, Renal, Anaemia, Bone).

    Energy for contraction

    • ATP powers the power stroke indirectly (hydrolysis cocks the head), detaches myosin from actin and drives SERCA to pump Ca²⁺ back.
    • Creatine phosphate: creatine kinase regenerates ATP instantly; lasts seconds (sprinting). Raised CK in blood means muscle damage.
    • Anaerobic glycolysis: fast, low yield, lactate; tens of seconds to minutes.
    • Oxidative phosphorylation: slow, high yield; endurance.

    Rigor mortis: after death ATP runs out, so myosin heads cannot detach and muscles lock.

    Fibre types and motor units

    • Type I (slow oxidative): thin, many mitochondria, dense capillaries, lots of myoglobin (red), fatigue-resistant. Posture (soleus).
    • Type II (fast glycolytic): large diameter, few mitochondria, glycogen-rich, fast myosin ATPase, tire quickly. Power (sprinting, eye muscles).
    • Motor unit: one motor neuron and all the fibres it supplies. Small units for fine control (eye, hand), large units for power (thigh).
    • Grading force: recruit more units (small first: size principle) and fire them faster (summation → tetanus).

    Myasthenia vs Lambert–Eaton

    Myasthenia gets worse with repeated use (receptors are scarce, so each impulse fails sooner). Lambert–Eaton gets briefly better with use (repeated firing builds up enough presynaptic calcium to release ACh).

    MSK · Pharmacology

    Drugs: NSAIDs, analgesia, bone and the NMJ

    NSAIDs are an MSK learning outcome. Learn one pathway, the arachidonic acid cascade, and every NSAID benefit and side effect falls out of it.

    NSAIDs: what they do and why

    • Analgesic: less PGE₂ sensitising nociceptors.
    • Anti-inflammatory: less vasodilation and oedema.
    • Antipyretic: less PGE₂ in the hypothalamus, so the temperature set point falls.
    • Antiplatelet (aspirin): less TXA₂.

    COX-1 is always on (constitutive): stomach, kidney, platelets. COX-2 is switched on by inflammation (inducible). Blocking COX-1 causes most of the side effects; blocking COX-2 gives most of the benefit.

    Side effects explained by the pathway

    • Stomach: less protective PGE₂ (mucus, bicarbonate, blood flow) → dyspepsia, peptic ulcers, GI bleeding. Give a PPI to high-risk patients.
    • Kidney: prostaglandins keep the afferent arteriole open → NSAIDs can cause acute kidney injury, especially with dehydration, ACE inhibitors and diuretics ("triple whammy").
    • Heart: fluid retention, raised BP, worsened heart failure; coxibs and diclofenac raise thrombotic risk.
    • Lungs: shunting arachidonic acid to leukotrienes can trigger bronchospasm in aspirin-sensitive asthma.
    • Blood: bleeding, especially with anticoagulants.

    Chapter 3 · Blood, clotting and repair

    Blood, haemostasis and wound healing

    Where blood cells come from, how bleeding is stopped in four acts (spasm, platelets, coagulation, fibrinolysis) with the brakes that keep a clot local, and how a wound heals from clot to scar. Every pathway can be hidden, dragged into order or tapped in sequence.

    Immune · Blood

    Blood cells and where they come from

    Every blood cell comes from one haematopoietic stem cell in red marrow. The first fork decides the career: the myeloid line makes red cells, platelets and most innate cells; the lymphoid line makes lymphocytes (B, T and NK cells).

    • Erythrocytes: biconcave disc (large surface for gas exchange, squeezes through capillaries), no nucleus or mitochondria, packed with haemoglobin. Live about 120 days, then removed by splenic macrophages; haem becomes bilirubin.

    • HbA1c reflects average glucose over about 2–3 months because red cells live about 120 days.

    Clinical link: chronic kidney disease causes anaemia because the kidney makes less EPO; it is treated with synthetic EPO.

    Blood · Haemostasis

    Haemostasis: stopping bleeding

    Haemostasis happens in four overlapping acts, each slower but stronger than the one before: vascular spasm (seconds), the platelet plug (primary haemostasis, minutes), coagulation (secondary haemostasis, minutes: fibrin glues the plug), and fibrinolysis (hours to days: the clot is removed once the vessel has healed). Natural brakes run alongside all four and keep the clot confined to the wound.

    Fixing a burst pipe. Most bleeding and clotting disorders are one of these steps failing, or one of the brakes failing.

    Act 1 · Vascular spasm

    Why spasm matters, and its limits

    • It buys time: less flow means platelets and clotting factors are not washed away.
    • It is strongest in small vessels. Capillaries have no smooth muscle and rely on the plug alone.
    • The same molecules have two jobs: TXA₂ and serotonin from platelets both constrict vessels and recruit more platelets.
    • The opposite signals, NO and prostacyclin (PGI₂), are what healthy endothelium makes all the time to keep vessels open and platelets moving.

    Clinical link: tourniquets and direct pressure copy what spasm does. Adrenaline in local anaesthetic causes vasoconstriction to reduce bleeding (and slow absorption).

    Clinical link · two bleeding patterns

    Platelet or vWF problem (primary haemostasis): bleeding starts immediately after a cut and is superficial and mucosal: nosebleeds, gum bleeding, heavy periods, petechiae, easy bruising. Clotting factor problem (secondary haemostasis): the plug forms but is not stabilised, so bleeding is delayed and deep: joints (haemarthrosis), muscles, after dental extraction hours later.

    Act 3 · Coagulation (secondary haemostasis)

    Coagulation is a cascade of serine proteases: each activated factor cuts and activates the next, so a tiny trigger is amplified into a flood of thrombin. Two models describe it. The classical cascade (intrinsic, extrinsic, common) explains the laboratory tests. The cell-based model (initiation, amplification, propagation) explains what happens in a real wound, and why haemophilia bleeds.

    Two things are needed for each big reaction: a cofactor (VIIIa for tenase, Va for prothrombinase) and a surface (negatively charged phospholipid on activated platelets, held together with Ca²⁺). This is why clotting happens on the plug and not freely in the blood.

    Vitamin K: why "1972" and why warfarin is slow

    • Factors II, VII, IX and X (and proteins C and S) need vitamin K to add γ-carboxyglutamate groups. These grab Ca²⁺ so the factor can sit on the phospholipid surface.
    • Warfarin blocks vitamin K epoxide reductase, so vitamin K cannot be recycled and the liver releases inactive factors.
    • Existing factors must clear first. Factor VII has the shortest half-life (about 6 hours), so the PT/INR rises first; prothrombin (about 3 days) clears last, so full anticoagulation takes several days.
    • Proteins C and S also fall early, so warfarin is briefly pro-thrombotic: heparin is overlapped when starting it for a clot.

    Thrombin: the foreman with many jobs

    • Cleaves fibrinogen to fibrin, and activates XIII to cross-link it.
    • Activates V, VIII and XI (positive feedback) and platelets through PAR-1.
    • Activates TAFI, which protects the clot from lysis.
    • When bound to thrombomodulin on healthy endothelium, it switches sides and activates protein C (negative feedback).

    Dabigatran blocks thrombin directly; heparin and LMWH act through antithrombin; the "-xabans" block Xa.

    The brakes: natural anticoagulants

    Virchow's triad: when the brakes are overwhelmed

    • Stasis: immobility, long flights, heart failure, atrial fibrillation (clotting factors pool and are not cleared).
    • Endothelial injury: surgery, trauma, cannulas, atherosclerosis (tissue factor exposed, brakes lost).
    • Hypercoagulability: cancer, pregnancy, oestrogen (combined pill, HRT), dehydration, inherited thrombophilia, antiphospholipid syndrome.

    Venous clots (DVT, PE) are fibrin- and red cell-rich ("red clots"): treat with anticoagulants. Arterial clots form on ruptured plaque and are platelet-rich ("white clots"): prevent with antiplatelets.

    Act 4 · Fibrinolysis

    Clotting tests

    • PT / INR tests the extrinsic and common pathways. Prolonged by warfarin, liver disease, vitamin K deficiency. The INR standardises PT for warfarin monitoring (target usually 2–3).
    • APTT tests the intrinsic and common pathways. Prolonged by unfractionated heparin and haemophilia A or B.
    • Platelet count and fibrinogen. D-dimer (a fibrin breakdown product) is sensitive but not specific: a negative result helps rule out DVT or PE when probability is low.

    Memory hook: "PeT" goes with the extrinsic "EX-pet", and Warfarin goes with PT ("WePT"). "Heparin goes with APTT" because both are longer words.

    Blood · Wound healing

    Wound healing: from clot to scar

    Four overlapping phases: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling. Each phase sends the signals that start the next one, so a problem early on (infection, poor blood supply, steroids) delays everything after it. Bone heals by the same sequence, which is why fracture healing looks so similar.

    Think of it as

    A building site. The scaffolding goes up (fibrin clot), cleaners clear the rubble (neutrophils), the site manager arrives and hires the trades (macrophages), plumbers lay pipes (new capillaries), builders put up quick plasterboard (type III collagen), roofers cover the top (keratinocytes), and over the next year the plasterboard is replaced with brick laid along the lines of stress (type I collagen).

    • Infection: prolongs inflammation; bacteria and neutrophil enzymes damage new tissue.

    • Haematoma, movement and tension across the wound.

    Chapter 4 · Immune and lymphatic biomed

    Immunity: from barriers to memory

    Follow an infection from the moment the skin is breached: lymphatics carry antigen to the nodes, innate cells and complement act within minutes, inflammation delivers the troops, adaptive cells arrive in days, antibodies and memory protect you for years. Finish with how it all ties together and what happens when immunity misfires.

    Immune · Lymphatic system

    The lymphatic system

    Three jobs: return leaked fluid and protein to the blood, absorb dietary fat from the gut, and carry antigens and immune cells to lymph nodes, where immune responses start.

    Lymphoid organs

    • Primary (where lymphocytes are made and trained): red bone marrow (all lymphocytes made; B cells mature here) and thymus (T cells mature here).
    • Secondary (where immune responses start): lymph nodes filter lymph; the spleen filters blood, removes old red cells and makes antibody to encapsulated bacteria; MALT guards mucosal surfaces: tonsils (Waldeyer's ring), Peyer's patches in the ileum, appendix.
    • Lacteals in intestinal villi absorb fat as chylomicrons; this milky lymph is chyle.
    • Lymph moves by the skeletal muscle pump, the respiratory pump, smooth muscle in vessel walls and one-way valves.
    • Lymphatics are absent from avascular tissue (cartilage, epidermis, cornea), the CNS (which has its own glymphatic and meningeal drainage) and bone marrow.
    Clinical link · oedema

    Oedema follows from Starling forces. Raised hydrostatic pressure: heart failure, DVT. Low oncotic pressure: low albumin from liver failure, nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition. Leaky capillaries: inflammation, sepsis, burns. Blocked lymphatics (lymphoedema): after axillary node clearance for breast cancer, radiotherapy, tumour, or filariasis (the nematode Wuchereria bancrofti, spread by mosquitoes, causing elephantiasis).

    Clinical link · nodes and spleen

    Nodes enlarge when they respond to infection (tender, soft) or are invaded by cancer (hard, painless, fixed). Cancers spread along lymphatics to the first draining node (the sentinel node), which is biopsied to stage breast cancer and melanoma. After splenectomy, patients are vulnerable to encapsulated bacteria (pneumococcus, meningococcus, Haemophilus) and need vaccines and sometimes lifelong antibiotics.

    The spleen: a lymph node for the blood

    • Red pulp filters blood: macrophages remove old, damaged or antibody-coated red cells and platelets (this is where ITP platelets and spherocytes are destroyed).
    • White pulp is lymphoid tissue around arterioles: T cells in the periarteriolar lymphoid sheath (PALS), B cell follicles beside them.
    • The marginal zone B cells make fast IgM against polysaccharide capsules without T cell help. Lose the spleen and you lose this, hence the risk of overwhelming pneumococcal, meningococcal and Hib sepsis.
    • After splenectomy (or in sickle cell disease, which destroys the spleen): vaccines (pneumococcal, Hib/MenC, MenB, MenACWY, annual flu), often lifelong penicillin V, and urgent treatment of any fever. A blood film shows Howell–Jolly bodies.
    Think of it as

    Lymphatics are the storm drains of the tissues, and lymph nodes are police checkpoints along them. Everything that drains from a tissue is inspected before it reaches the blood, so an infection in the foot is "reported" at the groin first.

    Immune · Innate immunity

    Innate immunity: fast and general

    Present from birth, acts in minutes to hours, recognises broad patterns shared by many microbes, and has no memory. Three layers: barriers, then cells, then proteins.

    Think of it as

    Innate immunity is the castle walls plus the guards who are already on duty. They cannot recognise individual enemies, but they know what an enemy in general looks like (PAMPs) and what a damaged castle looks like (DAMPs), and they respond the same way every time. Adaptive immunity is the special forces trained against one enemy: slower to arrive, but precise, and they remember.

    Barriers: the first line

    Physical

    • Skin: keratinised stratified squamous epithelium; constant shedding (desquamation) removes bacteria; tight junctions; dry, acidic surface.
    • Mucosa: mucus traps microbes; cilia sweep them away (the mucociliary escalator, damaged by smoking and cystic fibrosis); flow of urine, tears and saliva flushes them out.
    • Epithelial tight junctions in gut, lungs and airways.

    Chemical

    • Lysozyme (tears, saliva) breaks down peptidoglycan in bacterial walls.
    • Stomach acid (pH 1–3): proton pump inhibitors raise infection risk, including C. difficile.

    • Commensals compete for space and nutrients and make inhibitory substances, so pathogens cannot settle.

    How innate cells see microbes: PRRs and PAMPs

    • PAMPs (pathogen-associated molecular patterns) are molecules microbes cannot do without and humans do not make: LPS, peptidoglycan, lipoteichoic acid, flagellin, unmethylated CpG DNA, double-stranded RNA.
    • PRRs (pattern recognition receptors) are germline-encoded receptors that detect them. The best known are Toll-like receptors: TLR4 detects LPS (Gram-negative), TLR2 lipoteichoic acid and lipoproteins (Gram-positive), TLR5 flagellin, TLR3 double-stranded RNA, TLR7/8 single-stranded RNA, TLR9 CpG DNA.
    • PRRs also detect DAMPs (damage-associated patterns) from our own injured cells, which is why trauma alone causes inflammation.
    • PRR binding switches on NF-κB → inflammatory cytokines, and for viral patterns, type I interferons.

    Acute phase proteins (made by the liver, driven by IL-6)

    • CRP (C-reactive protein, a pentraxin): binds phosphocholine on microbes, acts as an opsonin and activates the classical complement pathway. Rises within 6–8 hours, peaks at about 48 hours; used to track infection and inflammation.
    • Mannose-binding lectin (MBL): binds mannose on microbes; opsonin; starts the lectin pathway.
    • Fibrinogen, serum amyloid A, ferritin rise; albumin falls (a negative acute phase protein).
    • ESR rises because fibrinogen makes red cells stack (rouleaux) and settle faster.

    Immunity · Inflammation

    Inflammation: acute, chronic, and how it ends

    Inflammation is how the innate immune system delivers cells and proteins from the blood to the place they are needed. Acute inflammation is fast (minutes to days), driven by fluid and neutrophils. Chronic inflammation lasts weeks to years, is driven by macrophages and lymphocytes, and destroys and repairs tissue at the same time.

    Think of it as

    An emergency response to a crash. The alarm sounds (mast cells, macrophages), the roads are widened (vasodilation), the barriers open (leaky vessels), ambulances slow down, pull over and the crews get out (neutrophils roll, stick and squeeze out), the clean-up team follows (macrophages), and finally the sirens are switched off (resolution). Every cardinal sign is a side effect of one of these steps.

    Five cardinal signs, explained

    • Rubor (redness) and calor (heat): vasodilation brings more blood.
    • Tumor (swelling): leaky vessels let protein-rich exudate into tissues.
    • Dolor (pain): bradykinin and prostaglandins sensitise nerve endings; swelling stretches tissue.
    • Functio laesa (loss of function).

    Outcomes: resolution, healing by scar, abscess, or chronic inflammation (macrophages, lymphocytes, fibrosis, granulomas).

    Arachidonic acid: where NSAIDs and steroids act

    Anti-inflammatory drugs at a glance

    • NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin): block COX → less PGE₂ and PGI₂. Reduce pain, fever, swelling. Risks: gastric ulcers, AKI, raised BP, asthma in some, bleeding (aspirin).
    • Paracetamol: mainly central COX inhibition; antipyretic and analgesic but barely anti-inflammatory.
    • Corticosteroids (prednisolone, hydrocortisone): switch off many inflammatory genes (NF-κB), inhibit PLA₂ and COX-2, reduce cytokines and cause neutrophils to stay in the blood (neutrophilia) while lymphocytes and eosinophils fall.
    • Antihistamines: block H₁ receptors; help itch and urticaria but not the airway or blood pressure in anaphylaxis.
    • Biologics: anti-TNF, anti-IL-6R, IL-1 blockers, anti-IL-17 (psoriasis). Infection risk, especially TB with anti-TNF.
    • Colchicine: blocks microtubules, so neutrophils cannot migrate (gout, pericarditis).

    Outcomes and chronic inflammation

    Four outcomes of acute inflammation

    • Resolution: little tissue damage, cause removed; lymphatics drain the exudate, macrophages eat dead neutrophils, tissue returns to normal (e.g. lobar pneumonia).
    • Healing by fibrosis: when lots of tissue is destroyed, or the tissue cannot regenerate (heart muscle after infarction).
    • Abscess: a walled-off collection of pus.
    • Chronic inflammation: when the cause persists (infection, foreign body, autoimmunity).

    Resolution is an active process, not just "running out": neutrophil apoptosis, efferocytosis by macrophages and lipid mediators (lipoxins, resolvins) actively switch it off.

    Chronic inflammation

    • Causes: persistent infection (TB, hepatitis B and C, H. pylori), prolonged toxic exposure (silica, smoking), autoimmunity (RA, IBD), unresolved acute inflammation.
    • Cells: macrophages (the central cell), lymphocytes and plasma cells, sometimes eosinophils.
    • Features: tissue destruction, attempted repair with fibrosis and new vessels, all happening at once.
    • Consequences: loss of function (cirrhosis, lung fibrosis), anaemia of chronic disease, amyloidosis, and a raised cancer risk (hepatitis → liver cancer, ulcerative colitis → colorectal cancer).

    Immunity · Complement

    The complement system

    About 30 plasma proteins, mostly made by the liver, that circulate as inactive precursors. When one is activated on a surface it cuts and activates the next, so a small trigger becomes a large response. It is innate (works in minutes without antibody), but it also "complements" antibodies, which is where the name comes from.

    Think of it as

    A long row of dominoes floating in the blood. Three different fingers can tip the first domino (classical, lectin, alternative), but they all knock into the same big domino, C3. After that the effects are shared: tag the microbe (C3b), sound the alarm (C3a, C5a), and punch a hole in it (C5b–9). Our own cells carry "do not tip" badges.

    Complement functions: "C3b binds bugs, C5a calls, C5b–9 kills"

    • Opsonisation: C3b coats microbes so phagocytes (with complement receptor CR1) engulf them.
    • Inflammation: C3a and C5a (anaphylatoxins) make mast cells degranulate.
    • Chemotaxis: C5a attracts neutrophils.
    • Lysis: the membrane attack complex punches holes, especially in thin-walled Gram-negative bacteria such as Neisseria.
    • Clearance of immune complexes to the spleen and liver.

    Clinical: C5–C9 deficiency → recurrent Neisseria infection. Early classical deficiency (C1q, C2, C4) → SLE-like disease. C1-inhibitor deficiency → hereditary angioedema (bradykinin-driven swelling, no urticaria, does not respond to adrenaline as well as anaphylaxis).

    Immune · Adaptive immunity

    Adaptive immunity: slow, specific, remembers

    T cells inspect peptides presented on MHC; B cells bind whole antigen and make antibodies. Helper (CD4) T cells conduct the orchestra; without them, neither CD8 killing nor good antibody responses happen, which is why HIV is so devastating.

    Think of it as

    An orchestra. Dendritic cells hand out the sheet music (antigen on MHC), CD4 helper T cells conduct (cytokines decide whether the piece is Th1, Th2 or Th17), CD8 T cells are the snipers who remove infected cells one by one, and B cells are the factory that turns out guided missiles (antibodies). Take away the conductor (HIV destroys CD4 cells) and everyone plays badly.

    Immunity · Antibodies

    Antibodies: structure, classes and how they work

    Antibodies (immunoglobulins) are the secreted form of the B cell receptor, made by plasma cells. Each one binds one exact shape (an epitope) and then calls in other parts of the immune system to deal with it. Most of what an antibody does depends on its tail (Fc), which is set by its class.

    Think of it as

    A Y-shaped adaptor. The two arms (Fab) are hands that fit one shape exactly. The tail (Fc) is a handle that phagocytes, NK cells, mast cells and complement grab. Changing class (IgM → IgG, IgA or IgE) swaps the handle but keeps the same hands, so the antibody keeps its target but changes what it can call in.

    The parts, and words you need

    • Two heavy and two light chains (κ or λ), joined by disulfide bonds. The heavy chain constant region decides the class: γ (IgG), α (IgA), μ (IgM), ε (IgE), δ (IgD).
    • Variable regions at the tips of both chains form the antigen-binding site (paratope). Three hypervariable loops (CDRs) on each chain make the actual contact.
    • Fab ("fragment, antigen binding"): the arms. Fc ("fragment, crystallisable"): the tail that binds Fc receptors and C1q. The flexible hinge lets the arms reach two epitopes.
    • Affinity: how tightly one binding site holds one epitope. Avidity: the total grip of all sites together. IgM has low affinity but 10 sites, so high avidity, like velcro: many weak hooks.
    • Papain cuts at the hinge into two Fab and one Fc; pepsin gives one F(ab′)₂.

    Antibodies in the clinic

    Reading serology

    • IgM positive: recent or current infection (IgM appears first and fades over weeks to months).

    Herd immunity

    • When enough people are immune, an infected person meets too few susceptible people for the outbreak to grow, so the unvaccinated (newborns, the immunosuppressed) are protected too.

    • Vaccine hesitancy is best met by listening, giving honest information and a clear recommendation (the GMC expects you not to let personal beliefs affect advice).
    Immunity · Putting it all together

    How it all ties together

    Innate and adaptive immunity are not two separate systems: they are one response that hands over in stages. Innate immunity holds the line for the first few days and tells the adaptive system what kind of threat it is facing. Adaptive immunity then sends back weapons (antibodies, cytokines) that make the innate cells far more effective.

    Think of it as

    A castle under siege. The walls and moat (barriers) stop most attackers. Guards on patrol (macrophages, mast cells) ring the alarm bells (cytokines). The militia rushes in (neutrophils). Scouts ride to headquarters with a description of the enemy (dendritic cells to the lymph node). Headquarters trains special forces against that exact enemy (T cells) and builds guided missiles (antibodies) that also make the militia's job easier. Veterans remember the enemy for next time (memory cells).

    Immune · When immunity harms

    Hypersensitivity, autoimmunity, immunodeficiency, transplants

    Hypersensitivity is a normal immune mechanism aimed at the wrong target or at the wrong strength. Gell and Coombs types: "ACID" (Anaphylactic, Cytotoxic, Immune complex, Delayed).

    Think of it as

    Friendly fire. Type I: an over-sensitive smoke alarm (IgE on mast cells) going off at toast. Type II: "wanted" posters (antibodies) stuck on your own cells. Type III: antibody–antigen grit clogging the filters (kidney, joints, vessels). Type IV: a slow-burning grudge held by T cells, taking two to three days to flare.

    Slide check · type I

    The All Biomed slide says "eosinophils bind FcεRI receptors". It is IgE that binds FcεRI on mast cells and basophils; re-exposed allergen cross-links two neighbouring IgE molecules and triggers degranulation. Eosinophils arrive later. It also lists Graves' and myasthenia gravis correctly as type II, but these act by stimulating or blocking receptors rather than by destroying cells.

    Autoimmunity

    • Loss of tolerance to self. Central tolerance deletes strongly self-reactive cells in the thymus (AIRE) and marrow; peripheral tolerance (Tregs, anergy without co-stimulation) controls the escapees.
    • Triggers: genes (HLA), molecular mimicry after infection (rheumatic fever after group A strep), hormones (far commoner in women), environment (smoking and RA).
    • Organ-specific: type 1 diabetes, Graves', Hashimoto's, myasthenia gravis, coeliac disease. Systemic: SLE, rheumatoid arthritis.
    • Evidence that a disease is autoimmune: autoantibodies or self-reactive T cells, response to immunosuppression, and transfer of disease by antibody (neonatal Graves' and neonatal myasthenia from maternal IgG crossing the placenta).

    Immunodeficiency

    • Primary (inherited): X-linked agammaglobulinaemia (no B cells: bacterial infections after 6 months, when maternal IgG fades); DiGeorge syndrome (no thymus: T cell deficiency, low calcium from absent parathyroids); SCID; chronic granulomatous disease; complement deficiencies.
    • Secondary (acquired, far commoner): HIV, chemotherapy, steroids and other immunosuppressants, diabetes, malnutrition, old age, splenectomy, chronic kidney disease.
    • The pattern of infection points to the broken arm: bacteria and capsulated organisms → antibody or complement; viruses, fungi, Pneumocystis → T cells; staph and Aspergillus → neutrophils.

    HIV

    • A retrovirus that enters CD4 T cells (and macrophages) by binding CD4 plus a co-receptor (CCR5 or CXCR4), then reverse-transcribes its RNA into DNA and integrates it.
    • CD4 count falls; below 200 cells/mm³ or with an AIDS-defining illness = AIDS: Pneumocystis pneumonia, oesophageal candida, TB, CMV, toxoplasmosis, Kaposi's sarcoma.
    • Transmission: sexual, blood (needles), vertical (pregnancy, birth, breastfeeding).
    • Antiretroviral therapy (three drugs from different classes) suppresses the virus. Undetectable = untransmittable (U=U). Prevention: condoms, PrEP, PEP within 72 hours of exposure (sooner is better).

    Transplant rejection

    • Hyperacute (minutes): pre-formed antibodies (ABO, anti-HLA) → complement, thrombosis. Prevented by ABO matching and cross-matching.
    • Acute (days to months): T cells react to donor HLA. Treated with more immunosuppression.
    • Chronic (months to years): vessel wall thickening and fibrosis; the main cause of late graft loss.
    • Graft-versus-host disease: donor T cells in a bone marrow transplant attack the recipient (skin, gut, liver).
    • Drugs: calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, ciclosporin) block IL-2 production; antiproliferatives (mycophenolate, azathioprine); steroids; basiliximab (antibody to the IL-2 receptor). Cost: infection and cancer risk.
    • Corneas and cartilage are rarely rejected because they are avascular.

    Viruses

    How viruses change: drift and shift (influenza)

    • Antigenic drift: small point mutations in haemagglutinin (H) and neuraminidase (N) accumulate each year, so antibodies fit less well. Causes seasonal epidemics and is why the flu vaccine is updated yearly.
    • Antigenic shift: two different influenza A viruses infect the same cell (often in a pig, the "mixing vessel") and swap whole genome segments, making a new H or N type that nobody is immune to. Causes pandemics (1918, 2009 H1N1).
    • Only influenza A undergoes shift, because only it has animal reservoirs.

    Viruses damage cells by lysis, by switching off host synthesis, by triggering immune killing of infected cells, and some by transformation into cancer (HPV, EBV, hepatitis B).

    Slide check · viral replication

    The "viral replication" slide describes a retrovirus (HIV): reverse transcriptase and integrase are not used by most viruses. A general cycle is attach → penetrate → uncoat → replicate genome and make proteins → assemble → release (budding or lysis). The retrovirus version is kept as its own pathway because the drug targets come from it.

    Fungi, protozoa, helminths, prions

    • Fungi: superficial (athlete's foot, ringworm), mucosal (oral and vaginal Candida, after antibiotics, steroid inhalers, diabetes), invasive (Aspergillus, Candida sepsis, Pneumocystis in the immunosuppressed).
    • Protozoa: malaria (P. falciparum can kill within days: any fever after travel to an endemic area needs malaria films), Giardia (diarrhoea after travel), Toxoplasma (cat faeces, undercooked meat; dangerous in pregnancy and HIV).
    • Helminths: threadworm (perianal itch in children), schistosomiasis (freshwater), filariasis (lymphatic blockage).
    • Prions: not destroyed by standard autoclaving; surgical instruments used on suspected CJD cases are quarantined or destroyed.

    Normal flora

    • Dense: mouth, large bowel (mostly anaerobes such as Bacteroides), upper respiratory tract. Moderate: skin (staphylococci, Cutibacterium acnes), vagina (lactobacilli). Sparse: stomach.
    • Normally sterile: blood, CSF, lower respiratory tract, bladder urine, joints, deep tissues. Any organism grown here matters.
    • Flora become pathogens when they move to the wrong place (gut E. coli into the bladder; skin staphylococci into a cannula site) or when the balance is upset (C. difficile after antibiotics).
    Infection · Pathogens and the response

    Pathogens, the response they trigger, and the symptoms that follow

    Each organism has a lifestyle (where it lives, how it spreads, how it hides). That lifestyle decides which arm of the immune system fights it, and the fight produces most of the symptoms. Learn the organism, the defence and the disease together and clinical questions become logical.

    Think of it as

    Symptoms are mostly the sound of the battle, not the enemy itself. Fever, aches, tiredness, swollen glands and pus are made by your own cytokines and white cells. That is why a patient with a weak immune system (neutropenia, steroids, old age) can have a severe infection with very few signs.

    Infection · Pharmacology

    Antimicrobials: antibiotics, antivirals, antifungals and antiparasitics

    Every antimicrobial relies on selective toxicity: hitting something the microbe has and we do not, or have in a different form. Bacteria offer many such targets; fungi and parasites are eukaryotes like us and offer few; viruses use our own machinery, so only virus-specific enzymes can be targeted. Learn one example per target and its classic side effect.

    Think of it as

    Sabotage. Bacteria are a rival factory with different machines (cell wall, 70S ribosomes, gyrase), so there is a lot to break. Fungi are a factory almost like ours, so only a few machines differ (ergosterol, the glucan wall). Viruses are squatters running their programme on our machines, so you can only break the few tools they bring with them: their key (entry protein), photocopier (polymerase), scissors (protease) and exit door (neuraminidase).

    Antibiotics

    Principles that come up in every question

    • Bactericidal (kills: β-lactams, glycopeptides, aminoglycosides, quinolones, metronidazole) vs bacteriostatic (stops growth, the immune system finishes the job: tetracyclines, macrolides, clindamycin, linezolid, trimethoprim). Cidal drugs are preferred when immunity cannot help: endocarditis, meningitis, neutropenia.
    • Time-dependent killing (β-lactams): what matters is time above the MIC, so give frequent doses or infusions. Concentration-dependent killing (aminoglycosides, quinolones): a high peak matters, so give once daily.
    • Spectrum: narrow (flucloxacillin: staph and strep; benzylpenicillin) vs broad (co-amoxiclav, piperacillin–tazobactam, meropenem). Broad drugs cause more C. difficile and resistance.
    • Penetration: some sites are hard to reach: CSF (need high-dose ceftriaxone), abscesses (drain them), bone, biofilms, inside cells (macrolides, doxycycline, rifampicin enter cells well).
    • Synergy: a β-lactam weakens the wall so an aminoglycoside can get in (endocarditis).

    Antivirals

    Why antivirals are harder than antibiotics

    • Viruses use host ribosomes and enzymes, so there are few virus-only targets.
    • Most antivirals are virustatic: they stop replication, and the immune system must clear infected cells.
    • Latent virus (herpes in ganglia, HIV in resting CD4 cells) is invisible to drugs: treatment controls but does not cure.
    • RNA viruses and retroviruses mutate quickly, so resistance appears fast unless drugs are combined (HIV: three drugs from at least two classes).
    • Timing matters: neuraminidase inhibitors work best within 48 hours of flu symptoms, before replication peaks.
    • Hepatitis C is the exception: direct-acting antivirals cure over 95% in 8–12 weeks, because HCV has no latent DNA form.

    Resistance and stewardship

    How resistance happens

    • Destroy the drug: β-lactamases, including ESBLs (extended-spectrum) and carbapenemases (CPE).
    • Change the target: MRSA makes an altered penicillin-binding protein (PBP2a); VRE alters the vancomycin binding site.
    • Pump it out: efflux pumps (tetracyclines).
    • Keep it out: fewer porins in the Gram-negative outer membrane.
    • Spread: mutation and selection, then horizontal transfer: conjugation (plasmids through a pilus), transformation (taking up free DNA), transduction (via bacteriophage).

    Antibiotics do not cause the mutations; they select the bacteria that already have them. Every unnecessary course widens the gap.

    Antimicrobial stewardship: Start Smart then Focus

    • Start smart: only start when there is evidence of bacterial infection; take cultures first; follow local guidelines; document the indication, drug, dose, route, duration and review date; give within 1 hour in suspected sepsis.
    • Then focus at 48–72 hours: review the diagnosis and results, then make one of five decisions: stop, switch IV to oral, change (narrow the spectrum), continue, or outpatient IV therapy.
    • Check allergy carefully: most "penicillin allergy" labels are side effects (nausea, diarrhoea), not true allergy, and wrongly force broader drugs.
    Immune · Prevention and control

    Preventing and controlling infection

    Infection spreads along a chain of six links. Every precaution you take on placement breaks one of them. Hand hygiene is the single most effective one.

    WHO "My 5 Moments for Hand Hygiene"

    1. Before touching a patient.
    2. Before a clean or aseptic procedure.
    3. After body fluid exposure risk.
    4. After touching a patient.
    5. After touching patient surroundings.

    Be bare below the elbows. Alcohol hand rub is the default, but use soap and water when hands are visibly dirty and for C. difficile or norovirus (spores and non-enveloped viruses resist alcohol). Gloves are not a substitute for hand hygiene.

    Standard infection control precautions (every patient, every time)

    • Patient placement and assessment of infection risk.
    • Hand hygiene; respiratory and cough hygiene.
    • Personal protective equipment matched to the task.
    • Safe care of equipment and the environment; safe linen and waste.
    • Safe sharps: never resheathe, dispose at the point of use.
    • Managing blood and body fluid spills; occupational exposure.

    Transmission-based precautions are added when needed: contact (C. difficile, MRSA: side room, gloves and apron), droplet (influenza: fluid-resistant surgical mask), airborne (TB, measles, aerosol-generating procedures: FFP3 respirator, negative-pressure room).

    Clean, disinfect, sterilise

    Cleaning
    Removes dirt and organic matter with detergent and water; must come first, because dirt shields microbes
    Disinfection
    Reduces microbes to a safe level but does not reliably kill spores (alcohol, chlorine)
    Sterilisation
    Destroys all microbes including spores (autoclave: steam at 121–134 °C under pressure). Prions resist it
    Asepsis
    Preventing contamination during procedures. Aseptic non-touch technique (ANTT): protect key parts and key sites

    Healthcare-associated infections

    • Organisms: MRSA, C. difficile, Gram-negative bacteraemia (E. coli, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas), CPE, norovirus.
    • Device and procedure infections: catheter-associated UTI, cannula and central line infections, surgical site infection, ventilator-associated pneumonia.
    • Prevention: remove devices as soon as they are not needed; daily cannula checks (VIP score); MRSA screening and decolonisation; antimicrobial stewardship; isolation.

    Needlestick or splash injury

    1. Encourage bleeding; wash with soap and running water; do not scrub or suck. Rinse eyes and mouth with water.
    2. Tell your supervisor and contact occupational health (or the emergency department out of hours) immediately.
    3. Risk assessment of the source; HIV post-exposure prophylaxis ideally within hours (up to 72 h) if indicated; check hepatitis B immunity.
    4. Report the incident.
    Clinical link · sepsis

    Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated response to infection (NICE NG51, updated 2024). Spot it early with NEWS2 and look for signs such as confusion, fast breathing, low blood pressure, mottled skin, no urine. Endotoxin and cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1) cause vasodilation, leaky capillaries and microthrombi: the innate response turned against the host.

    Immune · Thermoregulation

    Temperature control, fever and hypothermia

    Core temperature is kept at about 36.5–37.5 °C by the hypothalamus, which compares body temperature with a set point and adjusts heat production and loss. Fever moves the set point; hyperthermia overwhelms a normal one.

    How heat is gained and lost

    • Produced by metabolism (liver, brain, heart), muscle activity and shivering; non-shivering thermogenesis in brown fat (neonates) via uncoupling protein 1; thyroid hormone and adrenaline raise metabolic rate.
    • Lost by radiation (most at rest), conduction, convection and evaporation (sweating; the only route that works when the air is hotter than skin).
    • Sensors: peripheral cold and warm receptors in skin (cold signals travel in thinly myelinated Aδ fibres, warm in unmyelinated C fibres) and central warm-sensitive neurons in the hypothalamus.
    • Effectors: sympathetic nervous system (skin blood flow, sweating), somatic motor (shivering, behaviour), hormones (thyroid, adrenal medulla).

    • Mild 32–35 °C: shivering, confusion. Moderate 28–32 °C: shivering stops, drowsiness, bradycardia. Severe <28 °C: coma, arrhythmias (VF), may appear dead.
    • At-risk groups: neonates (large surface area to weight, cannot change behaviour), older people living alone, near-drowning (water conducts heat away fast), drug or alcohol overdose, trauma, hypothyroidism.
    • ECG: bradycardia, J (Osborn) waves, arrhythmias.
    • Management: remove wet clothes, insulate, warm fluids, active rewarming. In arrest: "nobody is dead until they are warm and dead".

      Sources · Corrections

      Where the slides need fixing

      Peer slides are useful summaries but contain errors that can cost marks. These are worth correcting in your own notes and Anki decks.

      MSK deck · ossification

      Intramembranous ossification forms flat bones (skull vault, clavicle), not the epiphysis. Endochondral ossification forms long bones, with a primary centre in the diaphysis and secondary centres in the epiphyses.

      MSK teaching deck · bone matrix

      Bone matrix is about two-thirds inorganic (hydroxyapatite) and one-third organic (mostly type I collagen) by weight, not the reverse.

      MSK deck · hypercalcaemia

      "Multiple melanoma" should read multiple myeloma.

      MSK deck · myasthenia gravis

      "Steroids e.g. pyridostigmine": pyridostigmine is an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor. Steroids are a separate immunosuppressive treatment.

      MSK deck · fracture healing

      A cast allows slight movement, so healing is secondary (callus). Primary (direct) healing needs rigid fixation with compression, such as plates and screws.

      Immune and All Biomed decks · Factor V Leiden

      Factor V Leiden is resistance to activated protein C, so factor Va is switched off too slowly and the patient clots too easily. It is not a lack of factor V.

      Immune deck · platelet plug and fibrinolysis

      Platelets adhere through vWF binding glycoprotein Ib (and collagen directly via GPVI and GPIa/IIa); ADP and thromboxane A₂ drive aggregation. The enzyme is tissue plasminogen activator.

      Immune deck · aspirin

      Aspirin irreversibly inhibits the COX enzyme (cyclo-oxygenase); there are no "COX receptors".

      Immune and All Biomed decks · NK cells

      NK cells do not use PRRs to look for MHC I. They use inhibitory receptors (KIRs) that are switched off when MHC I is missing ("missing self"), plus activating receptors for stress molecules and CD16 for antibody-coated cells.

      Immune and All Biomed decks · complement

      Only C5a is chemotactic. C5b starts the membrane attack complex (C5b–9).

      Immune and All Biomed decks · B cell activation

      The B cell presents antigen on its own MHC II to the helper T cell, which helps through CD40L–CD40 and cytokines. The slide describes the T cell presenting to the B cell.

      Adaptive lecture · T helper subsets

      IL-12 (not IL-2) drives Th1. Th2 mainly targets helminths and drives allergy; Th17 targets extracellular bacteria and fungi.

      Immune deck · interleukin mnemonic

      The mnemonic slide is headed "Th17" but is about interleukins 1–8 in general.

      All Biomed deck · type I hypersensitivity

      IgE binds FcεRI on mast cells and basophils; allergen cross-links it. Eosinophils do not bind FcεRI to trigger the immediate reaction; they join the late phase.

      Immune deck · viral replication

      The slide shows the retrovirus (HIV) cycle as if it applied to all viruses. Reverse transcription and integration are specific to retroviruses (and hepatitis B uses reverse transcription).

      Immune deck · PCR

      PCR amplifies DNA from any organism; RNA viruses need RT-PCR. It is not limited to DNA viruses.

      Immune deck · temperature

      "Hyperthermia >37.9 °C" conflates two things. Fever is a raised temperature from a raised set point; hyperthermia is a raised temperature with a normal set point.

      Adaptive lecture · IgA

      IgA is the most abundant antibody made each day (mostly into mucosal secretions), while IgG is the most abundant in serum. Both statements appear in different decks; know which one the question means.
